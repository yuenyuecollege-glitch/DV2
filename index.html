Volunteer organisations · Australia

Who still shows up?

Formal volunteering through organisations has slipped since before COVID — but Australians still give hundreds of millions of hours across sport, faith and community groups, and regional places lean on volunteers more than the big cities.

22.6% of adults did unpaid voluntary work through an organisation in 2025
618M formal volunteer hours in the prior 12 months — back above the 2020 dip
34% vs 21% remote Australia vs major cities formal volunteering rate

01 — Locate

Where people still volunteer

Participation is uneven. Census 2021 maps show regional SA4s with higher rates, while absolute headcounts pile into New South Wales and Victoria. The GSS remoteness gap is stark: remote Australia volunteers formally at 34%, major cities at 21%.

C1 · Choropleth — rates peak outside the capitals

Census 2021. Colour is the share of people 15+ who volunteered through an organisation. The Wheat Belt (26.8%) stands out; parts of Sydney SW sit near 6.8%. Equal-area Albers keeps region size comparable.

C2 · Proportional symbols — where the numbers sit

Same projection. Circle area encodes headcount. NSW and Victoria hold the largest pools — rates and counts tell different stories about where organisations find people.

C3 · Dot density — each dot is about 10,000 volunteers

One dot ≈ 10,000 Census volunteers, placed inside each state. Denser clusters show absolute scale for volunteer-involving organisations — not higher rates.

C4 · Radial bars — remoteness lifts the rate

ABS General Social Survey 2025. Bar length from the centre is the formal rate. Remote Australia hits 34.0% while major cities sit at 20.9% — a 13-point spread that organisations feel when recruiting outside the capitals.

Regional and remote communities still lean harder on formal volunteering than the capitals — even after a national decline.

02 — Identify

Who steps forward — and how that changed

Mid-life adults carry formal volunteering. People aged 45–54 sit at 28%; those aged 25–34 are at 13%. Since 2019, the formal organisation rate fell from 29.5% to 22.6% — a signal for how organisations recruit.

C5 · Lollipop — mid-life leads; young adults lag

GSS 2025 formal rates by age. Highlighted ends: peak mid-life at 28% versus the softest working-age band at 13%.

C6 · Waterfall — the formal rate stepped down twice

From 29.5% in 2019 to 24.8% in 2020 (−4.7 pts), then to 22.6% in 2025 (−2.2 pts). Organisation volunteering did not bounce with hours — the participation base stayed thinner.

03 — Compare

What they do — sectors and community activities

Among formal volunteers, sport and recreation organisations lead (33%), nested under sport & leisure alongside faith & learning and community life. Where people live also shapes who shows up formally: cities hold most of the headcount even when remote rates run higher.

C7 · Hierarchical treemap — sectors nest the organisation mix

Organisation types among formal volunteers (GSS 2025), nested by parent sector. Tile area is share of volunteers. Teal marks the leading home: sport / recreation at 33%. People can name more than one type, so shares need not sum to 100.

C8 · Sankey — remoteness feeds the formal pool

Four remoteness areas (left) flow to formal vs not (right). Flows are approximate adult shares: ASGS population weights × GSS 2025 rates. Major cities still supply most formal volunteers (~15 of every 100 adults), even though remote rates are highest.

04 — Summarise

Hours and contribution to Australian society

The participation rate stayed soft, but total formal hours recovered: 596 million in 2019, 490 million in 2020, 618 million in 2025. Older volunteers give more hours. Charities report about 3.77 million volunteers on their books — volunteering’s organisational footprint.

C9 · Heatmap — who gives the long hours

Among formal volunteers, share in each annual hours band by age. Darker cells show where contribution concentrates — people 65+ are most likely to give 100 hours or more (44%).

C10 · Line — total hours recovered after the COVID dip

National formal hours (millions). Highlight: the 2020 dip to 490M, then recovery to 618M in 2025 — contribution bounced even while the rate stayed softer than 2019.

C11 · Waffle — about 23 in 100, or 14 in 100?

Unit charts. Left: GSS 2025 formal volunteering (~23%). Right: Census 2021 (~14%), collected under COVID-affected conditions. Same idea, different instruments — do not splice them into one trend.

ACNC’s 2023 charity filings count about 3.77 million volunteers on organisation books — overlapping with, but not identical to, the ABS person surveys. That is the organised face of contribution.

05 — Explain

Why people don’t — and what organisations face

Among non-volunteers, “no time” (47%) and “not interested or no need” (36%) dominate. Volunteers themselves most often cite helping others and personal satisfaction. Time pressure and motivation sit on opposite sides of the same ledger for volunteer organisations.

C12 · Diverging bars — reasons for and against

GSS 2025. Right: reasons among formal volunteers — led by helping others (65%). Left: reasons among people who did not volunteer formally — led by no time (47%). People could choose more than one reason.

For volunteer-involving organisations, the message is plain: schedule flexibility fights “no time,” and a clear ask fights “never been asked” — while sport, faith and community groups remain the largest formal homes for help.
