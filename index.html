Volunteer Organisations

Formal volunteering through organisations has slipped since before the pandemic. Australians still give hundreds of millions of hours across sport, faith and community. Regional places lean on volunteers more than big cities.

Where people still volunteer

Participation is uneven across the map. Census 2021 shows regional areas with higher rates. Absolute headcounts pile into New South Wales and Victoria.

The General Social Survey remoteness gap is stark: remote Australia volunteers formally at 34%; major cities sit at 21%.

C1 · Rates peak outside the capitals

The Western Australia Wheat Belt is the darkest tile on the map, with a formal volunteering rate of 26.8%. That is the highest band outside the capital cities and shows how regional communities still show up when organisations need help.

Greater Sydney’s south west is the lightest capital city band, at only 6.8%. Place still shapes who steps forward: the same city region can hold both busy suburbs and neighbourhoods where formal volunteering stays soft.

C2 · Where the numbers sit

New South Wales alone holds about 858,000 Census volunteers, and Victoria about 712,000. Those two states carry the largest pools even though their rates sit mid pack rather than at the top of the ranking.

Rates and counts tell different stories for recruiters. Organisations hunting headcount look to the south east first, because circle size here tracks absolute volunteers, not the share of each state’s adult population.

C3 · Absolute scale across the states

Each dot stands for 10,000 volunteers. Denser clusters mark where people live in absolute numbers, which is a different story from where rates run highest on the choropleth.

Eastern states dominate the visual weight on this map. Remote Australia stays sparse in the scatter even when its formal rate is higher, so scale and rate need to be read together.

C4 · Remoteness lifts the rate

Remote Australia sits furthest from the centre at 34.0%, about 13 percentage points above major cities at 20.9%. Remoteness still lifts the formal rate after a national decline.

That gap is what organisations feel when recruiting outside the capitals. Regional and remote communities lean harder on formal volunteering even while the national rate has softened since 2019.

Who steps forward, and how that changed

Mid life adults carry formal volunteering. People aged 45 to 54 sit at 28%; those aged 25 to 34 are at 13%.

Since 2019, the formal organisation rate fell from 29.5% to 22.6%, a signal for how organisations recruit.

C5 · Mid life leads; young adults lag

People aged 45 to 54 sit at 28.0%, the peak mid life band for formal volunteering. That is still where organisations most often find people ready to give time through a group.

People aged 25 to 34 sit at only 13.1%, the softest working age band on the chart. Recruiting younger adults remains the harder ask for volunteer involving organisations.

C6 · The formal rate stepped down and stayed soft

The formal rate fell from 29.5% in 2019 to 24.8% in 2020, then eased further to 22.6% in 2025. The bar heights show a step down that never climbed back to the pre pandemic starting level.

Organisation volunteering did not bounce with hours. The participation base stayed thinner even as total hours recovered, so fewer people are carrying the formal load than in 2019.

What they do: sectors and community activities

Among formal volunteers, sport and recreation organisations lead (33%), nested under sport and leisure alongside faith and learning and community life.

Where people live also shapes who shows up formally: cities hold most of the headcount even when remote rates run higher.

C7 · Sectors nest the organisation mix

Sport and recreation is the leading home for formal volunteers, at 33.3%. It is the largest tile in the treemap and shows how clubs and physical recreation still anchor organised help.

People can name more than one organisation type, so the shares need not sum to 100%. Faith, community and other sectors sit beside sport rather than competing for a single exclusive home.

C8 · Remoteness feeds the formal pool

Major cities still supply most formal volunteers: about 15.0% of all adults flow from cities into the formal volunteer pool. That thick stream is why city headcount dominates even when city rates sit lower.

Remote Australia has the highest rate, yet only about 0.7% of all adults arrive as remote formal volunteers. Absolute formal capacity is still city led because population weight sits on the coast.

Hours and contribution to Australian society

The participation rate stayed soft, but total formal hours recovered: 596 million in 2019, 490 million in 2020, 618 million in 2025.

Older volunteers give more hours. Charities report about 3.77 million volunteers on their books. That is volunteering’s organisational footprint.

C9 · Who gives the long hours

Among volunteers aged 65 years and over, 43.7% give 100 hours or more in a year. That cell is the darkest on the heatmap and marks where long contribution concentrates.

Younger bands lean toward shorter hours, so contribution is not evenly shared by age. Organisations that rely on deep time still depend heavily on older volunteers.

C10 · Total hours recovered after the pandemic dip

Total formal hours fell to 490 million in 2020, the low point on the line. By 2025 they climb to 618 million, above the 596 million recorded in 2019.

Contribution bounced even while the participation rate stayed softer than 2019. Hours recovered faster than the share of adults who formally volunteer.

C11 · About 23 in 100, or 14 in 100?

The General Social Survey 2025 waffle reads about 23 in 100 adults volunteering through an organisation. That blue share is the survey’s formal participation picture for the latest year.

Census 2021 reads about 14 in 100 on the same idea with a different instrument and period. Do not splice the two panels into one trend; they measure related but not identical things.

Why people don’t, and what organisations face

Among non volunteers, “no time” (47%) and “not interested or no need” (36%) dominate.

Volunteers themselves most often cite helping others and personal satisfaction. Time pressure and motivation sit opposite on the same ledger for volunteer organisations.

C12 · Reasons for and against

Among formal volunteers, helping others or community leads at 65.2%, with personal satisfaction close behind at 62.8%. Motivation still sits firmly on the positive side of the ledger.

Among non volunteers, “no time” leads at 47.0%, and “not interested or no need” follows at 35.5%. Schedule flexibility is the main organisational fight on the against side.
