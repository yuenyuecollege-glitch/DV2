Who still shows up?

Formal volunteering through organisations has slipped since before the pandemic. Australians still give hundreds of millions of hours across sport, faith and community. Regional places lean on volunteers more than big cities.

01 — Locate

Where people still volunteer

Participation is uneven across the map. Census 2021 shows regional areas with higher rates. Absolute headcounts pile into New South Wales and Victoria. The General Social Survey remoteness gap is stark: remote Australia volunteers formally at 34%; major cities sit at 21%.

02 — Identify

Who steps forward — and how that changed

Mid-life adults carry formal volunteering. People aged 45–54 sit at 28%; those aged 25–34 are at 13%. Since 2019, the formal organisation rate fell from 29.5% to 22.6% — a signal for how organisations recruit.

C5 · Mid-life leads; young adults lag

The peak mid-life band carries formal volunteering — that is where organisations still find people.

The softest working-age band sits far below — recruiting younger adults remains the harder ask.

C6 · The formal rate stepped down and stayed soft

The rate fell through the pandemic year, then eased further — still below the 2019 starting level.

Organisation volunteering did not bounce with hours; the participation base stayed thinner.

03 — Compare

What they do — sectors and community activities

Among formal volunteers, sport and recreation organisations lead (33%), nested under sport and leisure alongside faith and learning and community life. Where people live also shapes who shows up formally: cities hold most of the headcount even when remote rates run higher.

C8 · Remoteness feeds the formal pool

04 — Summarise

Hours and contribution to Australian society

The participation rate stayed soft, but total formal hours recovered: 596 million in 2019, 490 million in 2020, 618 million in 2025. Older volunteers give more hours. Charities report about 3.77 million volunteers on their books — that is volunteering’s organisational footprint.

C9 · Who gives the long hours

People aged 65 years and over are most likely to give 100 hours or more.

Long hours concentrate among older volunteers — contribution is not evenly shared by age.

C10 · Total hours recovered after the pandemic dip

2020 is the low point; total hours then climb above the 2019 level.

Contribution bounced even while the participation rate stayed softer than 2019.

C11 · About 23 in 100, or 14 in 100?

General Social Survey 2025 reads about 23 in 100; Census 2021 reads about 14 in 100.

Same idea, different instruments — do not splice them into one trend.

Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission 2023 charity filings count about 3.77 million volunteers on organisation books — overlapping with, but not identical to, the Australian Bureau of Statistics person surveys. That is the organised face of contribution.

05 — Explain

Why people don’t — and what organisations face

Among non-volunteers, “no time” (47%) and “not interested or no need” (36%) dominate. Volunteers themselves most often cite helping others and personal satisfaction. Time pressure and motivation sit opposite on the same ledger for volunteer organisations.

C12 · Reasons for and against

Among formal volunteers, helping others leads — motivation sits on the positive side of the ledger.

Among non-volunteers, “no time” leads — schedule flexibility is the main organisational fight.

For volunteer-involving organisations, the message is plain: schedule flexibility fights “no time,” and a clear ask fights “never been asked” — while sport, faith and community groups remain the largest formal homes for help.
